# Completed next steps log — entry 73

73. [DECISION] **OPRF committee governance — who runs the committee that log #67 designed and log
    #69 built the circuits for.** Log #67 decided *what* the OPRF committee does (vOPRF over the
    passport personal-number field, threshold-split secret, checked via exclusion-proof at
    registration) and log #69 forked the circuits that consume its response, but both left *who
    actually runs it* as "TaceoLabs/oprf-service, self-hostable, no vendor lock-in" — a real
    constraint (not vendor-locked) but not a governance model. This entry resolves that, after
    several design iterations each pressure-tested by independent adversarial review — real
    structural problems were found and fixed at every stage, not just at the end, and that trail is
    worth keeping rather than only recording the final shape, since the rejected alternatives are
    exactly what the next person revisiting this needs to not re-litigate from scratch.

    ## Models considered and rejected before any committee design was attempted

    - **Fixed institutional committee** (Elections Commission, Judiciary, Audit Office,
      international monitors as seats) — accountable, immune to plutocratic capture, but not
      permissionless and requires real-world institutions to agree upfront. Not rejected as *wrong*,
      just set aside in favor of exploring whether something more decentralized was achievable —
      it resurfaces below as part of the actual design (the bootstrap phase), just not as the
      steady-state model.
    - **Open AGR-staked network** (anyone bonds AGR, large N, slashing for misbehavior) —
      permissionless, but lets capital buy influence over *voter eligibility itself*, a categorically
      worse thing to let money capture than ordinary block-production. Real-world evidence against
      this specific choice: World ID's own OPRF layer (built by TACEO, the same vendor whose
      `oprf-service` this project's circuits already target) explicitly did **not** choose staking
      for this exact role — it uses vetted institutional operators (a university, Nethermind,
      others) under a governance board instead. The team with the most resources building exactly
      this component made the same call this entry lands on.
    - **Reuse TACEO's live public OPRF network directly** — real, launched publicly ~March 2026,
      multi-tenant, and ZKPassport itself (this project's circuit vendor) is one of its seven listed
      node operators. Technically the fastest path to a working committee. Rejected as the long-term
      model because its governance is currently centralized and permissioned — both new node
      operators *and* new client applications need TACEO's discretionary approval to onboard — which
      would make Agora's voter-eligibility gate depend on a private startup's business continuity and
      approval decisions, the same vendor-lock-in shape already rejected once with Rarimo's
      `CertificatesSMT` registry (log #63). Kept on record as a legitimate **dev/bootstrap shortcut**
      for getting a first real end-to-end proof through `verifier.rs`, not as the production model.

    ## First real design attempt, and why an independent review found it broken

    The first fleshed-out design: a single sortition-selected committee of ~200 already-anchored
    citizens, reusing `pallet-courts`' existing delayed-reveal jury-selection pattern, running DKG to
    hold threshold shares. An adversarial review returned it not execution-ready, on grounds worth
    recording precisely rather than summarizing away:

    - `pallet-courts`' jury-reveal window (`JurySeedDelayBlocks`, `ConstU32<{10 * MINUTES}>` at
      `runtime/src/configs/mod.rs:447`) is the *wrong shape* to reuse — correct for jury
      unpredictability, far too short to coordinate a live multi-party DKG ceremony, and reusing it
      as-is would silently re-concentrate the committee toward always-on power users, defeating the
      point of sortition.
    - The corrupted-anchor-vs-threshold circularity (fraudulent duplicate-passport anchors, the
      disclosed residual gap from log #67, getting sortitioned onto the committee) was unbounded — no
      stated maximum tolerated corrupted-anchor fraction sized against threshold with real margin.
    - Accountability for sortition-selected ordinary citizens vs. a named institution was
      underweighted — cryptographic misbehavior (a leaked share, silent collusion) leaves no on-chain
      evidence trail the way an institution's public actions would.
    - No DKG/resharing protocol had actually been chosen, and — the decisive factual finding, not a
      judgment call — **no real production system runs threshold DKG or proactive resharing anywhere
      near 200 parties.** The actual deployed ceiling across every real system checked is ~15-40
      professionally-operated nodes: drand's League of Entropy (~15-19 organizations), Internet
      Computer's subnets (13-40 nodes), and — the closest precedent for the actual resharing
      primitive — Oasis Network's CHURP deployment, live on mainnet since July 2024. Academic papers
      claim far larger numbers (CHURP itself benchmarked to n=1001, one DKG paper to 8192), but only
      on reliable cloud infrastructure with dedicated bandwidth, explicitly excluding churn/dropout
      handling from the reported numbers — not a claim that transfers to intermittently-connected
      consumer devices.

    Two ways around the 200-party wall were proposed and checked, both dead ends worth recording so
    they aren't re-proposed without the reason already on file:

    - **Hierarchical/recursive composition** ("many groups of ~23, composed into one secret") — a
      literature search found no sound published construction for repeated threshold *function
      evaluation* with periodic resharing. Real hierarchical-threshold work (Tassa 2007, and a 2024
      follow-up, FlexHi) covers weighted *authorization* within one DKG/setup, not composition of
      independently-run sub-ceremonies. Internet Computer's own multi-subnet architecture looks like
      a precedent at first glance but isn't — confirmed directly against DFINITY's documentation that
      each subnet holds a fully independent key with **no** mechanism combining subnets into one
      result.
    - **Growing one committee incrementally via ordinary resharing** (bootstrap small, reshare to a
      larger `n` over time) — this part *is* real and sound (exactly what CHURP/Desmedt-Jajodia-style
      resharing to a differently-sized committee is for), but doesn't itself dodge the scale ceiling,
      since a resharing round's live-participation requirement still scales with the committee's
      *current* size at the time it runs.

    ## Where it landed: 5 independent committees, combined by hashed summation

    A structural restructuring, not a parameter change — replace one large committee with several
    independently-sized-to-precedent ones, combined algebraically rather than by routing:

    - **X = 5 independent committees, n = 35 members each** (target — see the realized-variance note
      below), internal threshold **t = 12** (~1/3). Each individually matches real deployed scale
      instead of one committee sized far outside it.
    - **Every registration query goes to all 5 committees symmetrically**, not routed to one via a
      hash. An intermediate idea — deterministically route each citizen to *one* of N committees via
      `H(national_id) mod N` — was tried and rejected: it solves cross-committee double-registration
      (same person always routes to the same shard) but leaks a public, low-entropy signal on *every*
      citizen's *every* registration, on the exact field this whole architecture exists to protect.
      Querying all 5 the same way for everyone removes that signal structurally instead of needing a
      cryptographic patch for it.
    - **Combination**: each committee's response is hashed (Poseidon2, domain-separated per
      committee — already used throughout this project for exactly this shape of fixed-arity hashing)
      before being summed across all 5: `final_anchor = Poseidon2(identity_input, tag_1, Y_1) + ... +
      Poseidon2(identity_input, tag_5, Y_5)`, field addition, all 5 in the same group. This gives a
      real, checked security property — the combined anchor stays unpredictable as long as even *one*
      of the 5 committees remains honest, even if all 4 others are fully compromised (a standard
      one-time-pad argument, sound in the random-oracle model). An earlier version summed raw,
      unhashed group elements directly; a review correctly flagged that real hybrid-cryptography
      combiners never do this (raw group elements carry algebraic structure combiner attacks can
      exploit — RFC 9497's own vOPRF spec has a dedicated `Finalize`-hash step for exactly this
      reason), and the Poseidon2 step closes that gap. On-chain authentication of this combination
      isn't a new mechanism to design — the `anchor` Noir circuit (log #69) already exists
      specifically to "verify the committee's response, emit the anchor" as a SNARK constraint;
      extending it to check 5 proofs and the summation instead of 1 is real circuit-engineering work
      but an application of an already-proven pattern in this codebase, not an open question.
    - **Disclosed cost, not hidden**: this is (n-of-n) combination, not threshold combination — *all
      5* committees must respond for a registration to succeed, unlike a Shamir scheme's partial
      fault tolerance. Modeled against a 48h SLA (registration is not a real-time flow, unlike
      voting), combined success stays roughly 90%+ even under a pessimistic 50%-per-member-
      reliability assumption; a failed attempt is a retry, not a lockout. This should be read as a
      best-case/independent-failure estimate — correlated unavailability (a national holiday, a
      platform-wide outage hitting multiple committees at once) is a known, disclosed limitation of
      the arithmetic, not something currently corrected for.
    - **Committee eligibility**: `committee_slot = H(date_of_birth) mod 5` (Poseidon2), computed
      once, permanent for the citizen's lifetime — structurally guarantees no citizen is ever
      eligible for more than one committee, true by construction rather than by enforcement. Two real
      corrections along the way, worth keeping on record: (a) hashing the citizen's *anchor* instead
      was tried and is wrong, because the anchor rotates at each scheme-version boundary (~4 years),
      which would silently reassign committee slots and reopen the exact disjointness problem this
      fix exists to prevent — the same reasoning rules out adding the rotation cycle's year as a
      second hash input, considered and rejected for the identical reason; (b) an earlier version
      hashed `(national_id, DOB)` together, which leaks a small signal on the actually-sensitive
      field for whichever citizens get selected onto a committee — dropping national ID and hashing
      DOB alone removes that leak almost entirely, since DOB is typically already known or
      discoverable for this system's own targeted-deanonymization threat model (a named journalist or
      dissident), unlike national ID.
    - **Scheme-version rotation** (the existing ~4-year cadence from log #67, or emergency rotation):
      the same 5 slots persist, but each committee mints an entirely new secret via fresh DKG rather
      than preserving continuity via resharing — consistent with how scheme rotation already works
      (citizens re-link old and new anchors via the existing dual-evaluation proof). This has the
      useful side effect of fully clearing any residual founding-committee correlation risk (below)
      the first time it happens, not just eventually.
    - **Bootstrap is necessary, not optional, and here's the precise reason**: sortition draws
      committee members from the pool of already-anchored citizens, but producing an anchor requires
      a working committee to process the registration — a genuine circular dependency with no way to
      start from nothing, the same shape as a blockchain's first validators not being selectable by a
      token-weighted vote before any tokens exist. Fix: **5 independent 7-person founding groups (35
      distinct individuals total, no person seated in more than one group)**, same institutional seat
      categories per group as the originally-considered fixed model (Elections Commission, Judiciary,
      Audit Office, 2 academic/NGO, 2 international monitoring), same 6-of-7 threshold per group. An
      earlier version used one shared 7-person founding committee for all 5 — a review correctly
      flagged this as collapsing "compromise all 5 committees independently" down to "compromise
      6-of-7 founders," for up to a 4-year window (a full election cycle in most democracies, the
      period the system is most attractive to compromise and least resourced to detect it) — judged
      acceptable for a testnet/pilot but **not for a real national launch**, which is why founding-
      group independence is now the default rather than deferred. Handoff to sortition triggers at
      `N_min = 50,000` anchored citizens **or** 3 years, with the minimum-N floor now applied to
      *both* arms (an earlier version left the 3-year arm unfloored, which could have triggered
      handoff over a dangerously small, easily-poisoned pool).
    - **Emergency rotation**: replaces the runtime's actual current `EmergencyRotationOrigin =
      EnsureRoot<AccountId>` (`runtime/src/configs/mod.rs:257,280` — confirmed in code, a real bare-
      root placeholder, not a hypothetical) with an AND of `pallet-emergency-council`'s existing 2/3
      supermajority and `pallet-courts`' existing Oracle endorsement, both required to reference the
      same incident hash and the same `committee_id` — scoped per-committee (`Option<u8>`, `None` for
      system-wide) rather than forcing all 5 committees through rotation when only one is
      compromised, which would be needless disruption for citizens depending on the other 4.
    - **Accountability**: bonded, non-transferable deposit plus a sworn on-chain declaration per
      committee member, forfeitable via `pallet-courts`' existing `CitizenConduct` case type on
      provable misconduct — reuses this project's jury-duty-style accountability precedent for
      ordinary, sortition-selected citizens rather than inventing a new mechanism.
    - **A realized-committee-size subtlety, checked rather than assumed**: if selection uses private
      VRF self-selection (each candidate independently checks their own eligibility, rather than a
      single public on-chain reveal), the realized committee size per cycle is naturally a random
      variable, not exactly 35 every time — Shamir-based DKG only needs whatever `n` actually shows
      up to be fixed and known before the ceremony runs, with `t` scaled to preserve the ~1/3
      fraction. At `N_pool ≈ 10,000` per electorate (from `N_min` split five ways via the DOB
      partition), realized size has SD ≈ 5.9 around the target of 35; checked across the resulting
      plausible range (n≈25 to n≈45, t scaled proportionally), the corrupted-anchor margin
      (`K/N ≤ 2%` design assumption) stays at 11+ standard deviations throughout, confirming
      `N_min = 50,000` is adequate for this structure rather than carried over unchecked from an
      earlier design iteration that assumed a single 200-person committee.

    ## What this doesn't resolve, on purpose — real future work, not deferred embarrassment

    - **Ceremony-time network-layer anonymity.** Non-interactive DKG (Groth's construction, confirmed
      as what Internet Computer's chain-key cryptography actually runs, rather than assumed) would
      reduce the live-negotiation exposure during resharing ceremonies, but doesn't provide anonymity
      by design — IC's own node registry is public on purpose. Closing this gap needs an added
      mixnet/delayed-broadcast transport layer on top, genuinely new engineering not attempted here.
    - **Real telemetry.** Every availability number in this design rests on assumed, not measured,
      per-member reliability. `t`, `n`, `X`, and the SLA window should all be treated as adjustable
      pending real data from an actual pilot, not fixed by this entry.
    - **No OPRF committee service exists yet, at any scale, under any of these models** — this entry
      settles governance/topology, not implementation. `PassthroughAnchorVerifier` remains a stub
      accepting every proof, exactly as log #68 left it. Standing up even a single `oprf-service`
      instance (key generation, the DKG/resharing ceremony, node operation) is unstarted.

    ## Rejected, on the record so it isn't re-proposed without the reason attached

    - A **fee paid by the registrant, split among the verifying committee** — more Sybil-safe than
      AGR staking (sortition eligibility is per-unique-anchor, not purchasable with money), but risks
      functioning as a poll tax on voting eligibility (the same objection already on record from the
      AI-judge-review fee discussion in log #67's rejected-alternatives), and separately, paying per
      approval creates a direct incentive to approve regardless of correctness. If revisited:
      treasury-funded flat rewards, not registrant-funded fees.
